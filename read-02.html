Blame the wind.

Across the tropical Pacific, steady winds called trade winds usually blow from east to west, from South America toward Asia. They push warm surface water toward our side of the ocean, so the sea around the Philippines and Indonesia is among the warmest on Earth.

As warm water piles up in the west, cooler water rises from the deep along the coast of South America to take its place. Warm seas heat the air above them, so clouds and rain gather over the warm west Pacific.

During El Niño, the trade winds weaken, and sometimes even reverse. The warm water drifts back east toward the middle of the Pacific and South America, and the clouds and rain move with it, away from us.

Ocean and air then feed each other: a warmer east Pacific weakens the winds further, which lets even more warm water spread east. That loop is why El Niño can last for months once it starts.

Rain follows warm water.
